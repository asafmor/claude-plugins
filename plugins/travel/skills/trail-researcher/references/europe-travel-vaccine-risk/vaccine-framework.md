# Vaccine Assessment Framework — European Travel

This file defines what evidence to look for. It does not contain permanent country recommendations. Verify current guidance every time the skill runs.

## 1. Routine vaccination baseline

The travel assessment should first identify gaps in routine protection that matter during travel.

### Tetanus / diphtheria / pertussis

Check:

- whether the primary series is documented/complete;
- date of the last tetanus-containing dose;
- whether current home-country or traveler guidance recommends a booster for the elapsed interval;
- whether remote travel with limited access to treatment changes booster advice under current guidance.

Do not imply that tetanus is uniquely European. The travel relevance comes from wound exposure and incomplete/outdated routine vaccination.

### MMR / measles

Check:

- documented MMR doses or accepted evidence of immunity;
- current measles transmission/outbreaks in the destination or transit countries;
- current travel-specific catch-up recommendations.

Do not assume childhood vaccination is complete without records when the answer depends on it.

### Polio

Separate:

- routine immunization status;
- traveler booster advice;
- temporary WHO/IHR recommendations;
- entry/exit certificate requirements.

### Varicella

Usually a routine/catch-up question, not a trek-specific vaccine. Include when the traveler lacks accepted evidence of immunity and current guidance makes catch-up relevant.

### Influenza / COVID-19

Consider current season, current public-health recommendations, age/risk group, mass gatherings, and active waves. Do not treat them as mandatory trek vaccines by default.

## 2. Tick-borne encephalitis (TBE)

TBE requires subnational assessment whenever possible.

Collect:

- exact route and regions/districts;
- locally acquired human cases and official risk areas;
- season;
- habitat: forest, forest edge, meadow, pasture, scrub, exposed alpine terrain;
- elevation, without using a hard “safe altitude” cutoff;
- hiking/camping/off-trail exposure;
- repeated future exposure.

Evidence priority:

1. destination national/regional public-health authority;
2. ECDC locally acquired TBE surveillance/risk information;
3. current traveler-specific guidance such as TravelHealthPro or STIKO/DTG;
4. WHO;
5. peer-reviewed evidence for unresolved local questions.

Do not use tick-vector presence alone as proof of TBE-virus transmission. Do not derive a traveler probability from resident incidence.

## 3. Hepatitis A

Check:

- current country-specific traveler recommendation;
- whether recommendation is for all unvaccinated travelers or selected travelers only;
- current outbreaks;
- food/water hygiene context;
- rural/basic accommodation or prolonged local travel;
- prior vaccination/documented immunity.

A country may have low overall risk but an active outbreak or a recommendation limited to specific traveler groups.

## 4. Hepatitis B

Check both geography and behavior.

Relevant exposures can include:

- new sexual partners;
- tattoos or piercings;
- medical/dental treatment;
- blood exposure;
- healthcare/laboratory work;
- contact sports or activities with significant injury risk when medical care may be needed;
- long-term/frequent travel;
- residence or close household exposure in higher-prevalence settings.

Do not recommend hepatitis B solely because a country has non-zero prevalence. Use the exact current traveler guidance and the user's exposure pattern.

## 5. Rabies

Check:

- whether terrestrial rabies is present in the country/region;
- bat lyssavirus risk even where terrestrial rabies is absent;
- planned contact with dogs, cats, wildlife, farms, animal rescue, veterinary work, or caves/bats;
- running/cycling and other activities that can increase encounters with dogs where current guidance recognizes that risk;
- age/behavioral factors such as children being less likely to report minor exposures;
- remoteness;
- reliable availability and travel time to modern rabies post-exposure vaccine and rabies immunoglobulin.

Pre-exposure vaccination can simplify post-exposure treatment but does **not** remove the need for urgent wound cleaning and medical assessment after a possible exposure.

If current authoritative sources disagree about animal risk or access to post-exposure care, state the disagreement.

## 6. Typhoid

In Europe, typhoid vaccination is generally itinerary-specific rather than routine.

Check:

- country-specific traveler guidance;
- food/water sanitation;
- visiting-friends-and-relatives travel;
- prolonged stays;
- remote/basic travel;
- active outbreaks.

Do not recommend it merely for hiking in a European country unless the actual destination/exposure supports it.

## 7. Meningococcal disease

Check when relevant to:

- current outbreaks;
- mass gatherings;
- close-contact living arrangements;
- specific occupational or medical risk;
- country/event entry rules.

Distinguish MenACWY, MenB, or other formulations according to current guidance rather than using “meningitis vaccine” generically.

## 8. Cholera

Usually not relevant to ordinary European tourism or trekking. Consider only when current destination guidance identifies a specific outbreak/high-risk sanitation exposure or a special occupational/humanitarian context.

## 9. Yellow fever

Yellow fever is not an endemic European trekking risk, but certificate rules may become relevant because of the traveler's origin or transit through a yellow-fever-risk country.

Always separate:

- vaccination for medical risk at a non-European itinerary segment;
- certificate requirement for entry into a European or onward destination.

## 10. BCG / tuberculosis

BCG is not a normal adult European trek vaccine. Consider only when current national traveler guidance identifies prolonged/high-risk exposure, usually related to age, long stay, healthcare work, close contact, or high-incidence settings.

## 11. Mpox

Do not recommend mpox vaccine for European travel in general. Check current outbreak advice and whether the traveler belongs to an exposure group for whom vaccination is currently recommended.

## 12. New or unusual vaccine recommendations

The skill must remain open-ended. If current destination guidance lists another vaccine or an outbreak creates a new indication, investigate it even if it is not named above.

For every unexpected vaccine:

1. verify the recommendation with an authoritative source;
2. determine whether it applies to all travelers or a defined subgroup;
3. map it to the user's itinerary/exposure;
4. check timing and schedule from current official guidance;
5. state confidence and any uncertainty.
