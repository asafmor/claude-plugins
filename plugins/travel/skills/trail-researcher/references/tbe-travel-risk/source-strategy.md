# Source Strategy for TBE Travel-Risk Assessment

Use this file whenever researching a destination. Source names and websites can change, so verify that each source is current before relying on it.

## 1. Source hierarchy

Use the highest available level that answers the specific question.

### Tier 1 — Destination public-health authority

Prefer the country's national public-health institute, ministry of health, or the regional authority that owns TBE surveillance. Look for:

- official TBE/FSME risk maps;
- locally acquired case counts or incidence by district/region;
- annual surveillance bulletins;
- vaccination-area definitions;
- travel-health advice;
- current alerts or newly recognized foci.

Examples of authoritative bodies include:

- Germany — Robert Koch Institute (RKI)
- Sweden — Folkhälsomyndigheten
- Finland — Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (THL)
- Switzerland — Federal Office of Public Health / BAG-FOPH
- France — Santé publique France
- Italy — Istituto Superiore di Sanità (ISS/EpiCentro) and Ministero della Salute; also relevant regional health authorities
- Slovenia — National Institute of Public Health (NIJZ)
- Greece — National Public Health Organization (EODY)
- Austria — Austrian public-health authorities such as AGES and the Ministry/official vaccination guidance

This list is a starting point, not a whitelist. For another country, identify its equivalent public-health authority.

### Tier 2 — ECDC for EU/EEA destinations

Use ECDC for cross-country and regional comparison, especially:

- locally acquired confirmed TBE case notification maps by NUTS region;
- Annual Epidemiological Reports;
- tick distribution maps as ecological context only.

Starting pages:

- https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/infectious-disease-topics/tick-borne-encephalitis/surveillance-and-updates/locally-acquired-tbe
- https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/publications-data/monitoring/all-annual-epidemiological-reports
- https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/disease-vectors/surveillance-and-disease-data/tick-maps

Important: ECDC explicitly notes that tick-distribution maps show vector presence, not TBE-virus transmission, and do not represent each country's official position.

### Tier 3 — CDC traveler guidance

Use CDC for traveler-oriented risk framing and country-specific summaries:

- https://www.cdc.gov/tick-borne-encephalitis/data-maps/
- https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travel-associated-infections-diseases/tick-borne-encephalitis.html
- https://www.cdc.gov/tick-borne-encephalitis/hcp/vaccine/index.html

CDC is especially useful for combining location, rurality, season, duration, and planned outdoor activity into traveler vaccination decisions.

### Tier 4 — WHO

Use WHO for broad vaccination policy and disease-level guidance:

- https://www.who.int/teams/immunization-vaccines-and-biologicals/policies/position-papers/tick-borne-encephalitis

WHO supports risk assessment at country, regional, or district level and vaccination for travelers from non-endemic areas when visits to endemic areas include extensive outdoor activity. Verify the newest WHO material available at execution time.

### Tier 5 — Peer-reviewed research

Use recent peer-reviewed work when official sources leave a material gap, for example:

- newly emerging TBE foci;
- fine-scale regional studies;
- tick infection-prevalence studies;
- altitude/ecology work;
- traveler-specific attack-rate research.

Do not let a small ecological study override newer official human surveillance without explaining the difference.

### Tier 6 — Secondary sources

Travel clinics, hiking sites, news articles, Wikipedia, blogs, and tourism pages may help locate a route or explain terminology. They should rarely determine the final TBE risk classification when primary sources exist.

## 2. Search strategy

For each destination, run searches combining the disease name, exact region, year, and official authority.

Useful patterns:

- `[country] TBE risk map 2026 public health`
- `[region/district] tick-borne encephalitis cases 2025 2026`
- `[local-language term for TBE] [region] risk map`
- `site:[official-domain] TBE [region]`
- `site:[official-domain] FSME [region]`
- `site:[official-domain] encephalitis tick [region]`

Search the local-language disease term where possible. Examples include `FSME` in German-speaking countries and `encefalite da zecche` in Italy.

## 3. What to extract from each epidemiological source

Record:

- authority;
- page/report title;
- publication or update date;
- geographic unit;
- whether cases are locally acquired;
- period covered;
- case count and/or incidence if provided;
- official risk category if provided;
- vaccination recommendation if provided;
- caveats about surveillance or mapping.

If a map is image/PDF based, inspect the map itself rather than relying only on a search snippet.

## 4. Freshness rules

- Prefer current-year risk maps and alerts.
- Prefer the most recent complete surveillance year for incidence.
- For trends, use several recent years rather than one anomalous year.
- If the latest local data are older than 3 years, explicitly mention this and lower confidence unless another current authoritative source confirms the status.
- Always check for a current-year outbreak, newly recognized focus, or change in risk-area designation.

## 5. Interpreting human incidence carefully

Population incidence is useful evidence of local activity, but it is not the traveler's infection probability.

Reasons include:

- resident vaccination coverage;
- different amounts of outdoor exposure;
- underdiagnosis and reporting differences;
- imported versus locally acquired cases;
- population denominators that do not reflect people actually entering tick habitat;
- focal transmission inside a much larger administrative unit.

Use incidence comparatively and descriptively unless an authoritative source itself defines a risk threshold.

Example: Germany's RKI uses a formal district risk-area method based on multi-year incidence and statistical criteria. Preserve RKI's official classification rather than inventing a separate threshold.

## 6. Route and habitat evidence

Use route sources to establish exposure context, not disease incidence.

Useful evidence:

- official park/trail pages;
- GPX/topographic data;
- established route databases;
- satellite/topographic maps where supported;
- stage descriptions that identify forest, meadow, pasture, scrub, or exposed alpine terrain.

If exact habitat proportions cannot be established, describe them qualitatively and lower confidence rather than fabricating percentages.

## 7. Current reference facts to re-check during execution

These are methodological anchors, not permanent constants. Verify current wording before quoting them:

- CDC: TBE transmission is highly variable by place and over time.
- CDC/ACIP: highest traveler exposure occurs in endemic areas during the main transmission season when people undertake outdoor activities in woodland/tick habitat.
- CDC: activities can matter more than total time abroad.
- CDC: recognized transmission has expanded to higher altitudes, with foci reported up to about 2,100 m.
- ECDC: locally acquired case maps are preferable to vector maps for disease-risk evidence.
- WHO: vaccination policy should reflect local endemicity and target travelers with extensive outdoor exposure in endemic areas.

## 8. Minimum evidence standard for a confident answer

Aim for at least:

1. one current national/regional public-health source for the exact destination or nearest available geographic level; and
2. one independent authoritative cross-check such as ECDC or CDC.

If that standard cannot be met, continue the assessment but label confidence as medium or low and explain why.
